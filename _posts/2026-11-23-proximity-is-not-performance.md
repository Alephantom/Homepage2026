Remote work helped me tremendously. When we decided we wanted to give our children the best possible future, we moved to a suburb. Without the flexibility of remote work, I could not do the things I love with the quality and care I put into them today.

One story is not data. But in nine years in HR I have seen the same pattern too often to treat it as private: flexibility is what keeps many mothers and carers in good jobs at all. My worry is that two trends now pull against them. AI is making visibility the currency of a career, and return-to-office is tying visibility back to a desk. The people who can least afford that combination are the ones with a second shift at home.

## Why flexibility matters for mothers

In the US, 32 percent of mothers of young children worked remotely in 2025, compared with 28 percent of women and 20 percent of men without children.[1]

That doesn't surprise me. For a lot of mothers, remote work is what makes a full job possible at all: no long commute, and a sick child doesn't automatically mean a lost day. Flexibility didn't fix the motherhood penalty. For many of us, it stopped us from falling further into it.

## AI changes what gets noticed

Three in four knowledge workers worldwide already use AI at work, according to the 2024 Work Trend Index by Microsoft and LinkedIn.[2]

When a tool can draft the report and summarise the meeting, routine output stops proving much. What's left to show is judgment, ideas, relationships and the ability to move a decision. Those only count if someone sees them. So I think we will all be nudged to be more visible, to build a personal brand, and to show what we can do rather than simply do it.

Visibility takes time and presence. It favours people who can stay for the after-work drinks, catch the CEO in the corridor and take the spontaneous call at 6 pm.

## Meanwhile, the office is back

In the Women in the Workplace 2025 study by Lean In and McKinsey, one in four companies scaled back or ended remote and hybrid options that year. It was the biggest rollback of any policy they track.[3]

Return-to-office is often sold as a way to lift performance. I'm not convinced the evidence is there. The best study I know is a randomised trial published in Nature, run at Trip.com, a large Chinese travel tech company. Hybrid work cut resignations by a third, and over the next two years the researchers found no difference in promotions between hybrid and office staff.[4] One company doesn't settle the question, but it's hard to read that as proof that good work needs a desk every day.

My view is that proximity bias keeps a lot of mandates alive. In Owl Labs' 2024 survey of US workers, 55 percent said their managers see people in the office as harder working and more trustworthy than remote colleagues.[5]

## Who pays for it

The cost doesn't land evenly. In Women in the Workplace 2025, about a third of women who work mostly remotely had been promoted or had a sponsor in the last two years. For women who work mostly on-site, it was more than half. For men, it made little difference where they worked.[3] That's not proof that remote work holds women back, but it shows who gets noticed.

When the office comes back by mandate, women also seem more likely to leave. A large study of LinkedIn data from tech and finance firms found that turnover after mandates rose most among women, senior and skilled employees, and more of those who left took lower-ranked jobs.[6] If that holds, people are trading career steps for flexibility.

The national numbers worry me too. In the first half of 2025, US mothers of children under five saw the sharpest January-to-June drop in labour force participation since the data series began in 1989: 2.8 percentage points in six months.[1] Nobody has shown that return-to-office caused this, and childcare is a big part of the story. Mothers of young children are far more likely than fathers to say childcare problems are the main reason they are out of work, working part-time or missing work.[7] Still, the timing fits the pattern I'm worried about.

One more detail from the Nature study: women were less likely to volunteer for the hybrid option, and the authors suggest they may worry more about how working from home looks for their careers.[4] If that's right, the penalty may already be working before anyone says a word.

Here is my forecast. If visibility becomes the main way to prove value, and it's measured in office hours and hallway moments, then mothers, carers and anyone with less flexible time will be seen less, promoted less and will leave more often. It will happen quietly, one resignation at a time, without anyone ever deciding it should.

## What companies can do

I'm not against offices; some conversations really are better in person. I'm for designing visibility on purpose instead of leaving it to whoever happens to be in the room.

- **Measure outcomes, not presence.** Agree on goals at the start of a cycle and review against them.
- **Design fair visibility.** Rotate who presents to leadership. Treat async formats, like recorded demos or written updates, as equal to a live presentation. Keep a shared log of wins.
- **Co-create hybrid rules with carers.** If you set anchor days, set them with the people who manage school pick-ups and care appointments, not around them.
- **Let HR be the corrective.** In calibration rounds, ask out loud whether a rating reflects output or how often a manager sees someone.
- **Track the numbers.** Compare promotion rates, ratings and attrition by work arrangement. Where it is legal and voluntary (in Germany, agreed with the works council), look at caregiving status too, anonymised.

## A call to HR and leadership

Remote work gave many mothers and carers a way to stay in demanding jobs without breaking. AI and return-to-office don't have to take that away. They will if we let visibility default back to physical presence and call it performance.

If you lead a team or run HR, look at who you saw this quarter and who did the work. They aren't always the same people.

This is work I do with teams: hybrid rules, review processes and visibility formats that see the people doing the work, not just the people in the room. If you are thinking about this in your company, I'm happy to compare notes.
