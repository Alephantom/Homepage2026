Remote work helped me tremendously. When we decided we wanted to give our children the best possible future, we moved to a suburb. Without the flexibility of remote work, I could not do the things I love with the quality and care I put into them today.

One story is not data. But in nine years in HR I have seen the same pattern too often to treat it as private: flexibility is what keeps many mothers and carers in good jobs at all. My worry is that two trends now pull against them: AI is making visibility the currency of a career, and return-to-office is tying visibility back to a desk. The people who can least afford that combination are the ones with a second shift at home.

A note on reading this piece: the numbers are evidence and linked in the sources at the end. The forecast is mine.

## What flexibility did for mothers

In the US, the labour force participation rate of mothers whose youngest child is under six rose from 66.4 percent in 2019 to 68.9 percent in 2023.[1] The Hamilton Project found that in 2023, participation among women with young children had passed its pre-pandemic peak by more than 1.4 percentage points, and that highly educated, married mothers were much more likely than average to still telework weekly.[2] Mothers of young children remain more likely to work remotely than other groups: 32 percent in 2025, compared with 28 percent of women without children and 20 percent of men without children.[3]

In Germany, before the pandemic, in jobs that were well suited to home office, 58 percent of men but only 40 percent of women worked from home at least occasionally. During the pandemic, women in these jobs almost caught up.[4] A 2025 survey of graduates in 40 countries found that the desire to work from home is highest among women with children.[8]

None of this erased the motherhood penalty. In 2025, only 39.7 percent of German mothers with a child under three were in paid work, compared with 88.7 percent of fathers in the same situation.[5] Once reduced hours and lower pay are counted, the long-run earnings loss that comes with parenthood for women in Germany is around 61 percent, based on mothers whose first child was born between 1989 and 2005.[6] In the US, parenthood reduces women's earnings by 33 percent relative to men.[7] Flexibility did not fix this gap. It stopped many of us from falling further into it.

## AI changes what gets noticed

In the 2024 Work Trend Index by Microsoft and LinkedIn, 75 percent of knowledge workers said they use AI at work, and 66 percent of leaders said they would not hire someone without AI skills.[9] More than half of AI users (53 percent) worry that using it on important tasks makes them look replaceable.[9]

That last number stays with me. My reading, and this is opinion, not a finding: when a tool can draft the report, summarise the meeting and build the first version of the slide deck, the routine output stops proving much. What is left to show is judgment, ideas, relationships and the ability to move a decision. Those only count if someone sees them. So everyone is nudged to be more visible, to build a personal brand, and to show what they can do rather than simply do it.

Visibility takes time and presence. It favours people who can stay for the after-work drinks, catch the CEO in the corridor and take the spontaneous call at 6 pm.

## Meanwhile, the office is back

According to the Flex Index, two-thirds of US firms still offer location flexibility, but 34 percent now require full-time office presence, a 2-point increase from the previous year.[10] Among the Fortune 100, 45 percent require four or five office days a week.[10] The Women in the Workplace 2025 study found that one in four companies discontinued or scaled back remote and hybrid options that year, the largest rollback of any HR policy they track.[12]

Mandates are usually justified with performance. The evidence for that is thin. In a randomised trial with 1,612 employees, published in Nature, hybrid work cut quit rates by a third, with a significant effect for women, and the researchers found no evidence of a difference in promotions over the following two years.[14] My view is that proximity bias keeps many mandates alive. In Owl Labs' 2024 survey, 55 percent of employees said their managers see people in the office as harder working and more trustworthy than remote colleagues.[11] That is down from 63 percent the year before, but still more than half.[11]

## Who pays for it

The cost does not land evenly. In Women in the Workplace 2025, only a third of women who work mostly remotely had been promoted in the last two years or had a sponsor, compared with over half of women who work mostly on-site. For men, outcomes were relatively similar wherever they worked.[12]

When the office comes back by mandate, women are more likely to leave. A study tracking over 3 million tech and finance workers on LinkedIn found that turnover after return-to-office mandates rose more for female, senior and skilled employees, and that more of the people who left took lower-ranked positions at their next employer.[13] People are giving up career steps to keep the flexibility their lives depend on.

The national numbers worry me. In the first half of 2025, mothers of young children in the US saw the sharpest January-to-June drop in labour force participation in more than 40 years of data, 2.8 percentage points in six months.[3] For the full year, participation of mothers with children under six was 68.0 percent, down from 68.9 percent in 2023.[1] Return-to-office is not proven to be the cause, and childcare costs play a part, but the direction fits the pattern.

One more detail from the Nature study: women were less likely to volunteer for the hybrid option, and the authors suggest women may worry more about the career signal of working from home.[14] So the penalty works before anyone says a word: people self-select out of flexibility because they expect to be judged for it.

Here is my forecast. If visibility becomes the main way to prove value in the AI era, and visibility is measured in office hours and hallway moments, then mothers, carers and anyone with less flexible time will be seen less, promoted less and will leave more often. It will happen quietly, one resignation at a time, without anyone ever deciding it should.

## What companies can do

I'm not against offices; some conversations really are better in person. I'm for designing visibility on purpose instead of leaving it to whoever happens to be in the room.

- **Measure outcomes, not presence.** Agree on goals at the start of a cycle and review against them. If a rating can't be explained by results, it shouldn't be in the rating.
- **Design fair visibility.** Rotate who presents work to leadership. Offer async formats, like short recorded demos or written updates, and treat them as equal to a live presentation. Keep a shared log of wins so contributions are documented rather than remembered.
- **Co-create hybrid rules with carers.** If you set anchor days, set them with the people who manage school pick-ups and care appointments, not around them. Predictable schedules and fair notice matter more than the exact number of days.
- **Let HR be the corrective.** In calibration rounds, ask out loud whether a rating reflects output or how often a manager sees someone. Train managers on proximity bias before review season, not after.
- **Track the numbers.** Compare promotion rates, ratings and attrition by work arrangement. Where it is legal and voluntary, and in Germany agreed with the works council and data protection, look at caregiving status too, in aggregated and anonymised form.

## A call to HR and leadership

Remote work gave many mothers and carers a way to stay in demanding jobs without breaking. AI and return-to-office don't have to take that away. They will if we let visibility default back to physical presence and call it performance.

If you lead a team or run HR, look at who you saw this quarter and who did the work. They aren't always the same people.

This is work I do with teams: hybrid rules, review processes and visibility formats that see the people doing the work, not just the people in the room. If you are thinking about this in your company, I'm happy to compare notes.
